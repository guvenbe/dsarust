Bubble, merge and quick — line by line

Three sorting algorithms from one Rust file, each walked through a line at a time. Every trace below is generated by running the same steps your code runs, on the arrays from your own tests.

The three functions differ in more than speed — look at their signatures first, because that difference is really about ownership:

FunctionSignatureWhat it does to your data
bubble_sort&mut [T]Borrows your slice and rearranges it in place. You keep the Vec.
merge_sortVec<T> -> Vec<T>Consumes your vector and hands back a new one. The original is moved away.
quick_sort&mut [T]Borrows and rearranges in place, like bubble.

That is why the tests read bubble_sort(&mut v); but let v = merge_sort(v); — the second one has to rebind, because the old v no longer exists.

01bubble_sort

Walk the list comparing neighbours. Swap any pair that is out of order. Each full pass drags the largest remaining value to the right end — like a bubble rising.

Cost

  
O(n²) — up to n passes of up to n comparisons. For 1,000 items that is about a million comparisons. The sorted flag makes the best case (an already-sorted list) O(n): one pass, no swaps, return.

Why .take() and ref are there

    
ref a_val borrows the value inside the Option so you can compare it without moving it out — you are only peeking, the card must stay put. Then .take() is what lets you finally move it: it swaps the Option to None and hands you the contents. Without it the compiler would reject moving a value out of something you only borrowed.

  


  
Cost

  
O(n log n) — the tree is log₂(n) levels deep and each level merges all n items. Your comment on line 22 puts it well: for 1024 items that is 1024 × 10 instead of 1024 × 1024, roughly 100× less work than bubble sort. The cost is memory: unlike the other two, this one allocates new vectors.














  
03quick_sort

  
Pick one item — the pivot. Shove everything smaller to its left and everything bigger to its right. The pivot is now in its final position forever. Repeat on the left chunk and the right chunk.

Why split_at_mut and not two slices

    
You cannot normally hold two &mut into one collection — the borrow checker rejects it (error E0499). split_at_mut is the escape hatch: it proves to the compiler that the two halves do not overlap, so both mutable borrows are safe. Skipping b[0] matters twice over: the pivot is already final, and it guarantees each recursive call gets a strictly smaller slice, so the recursion terminates.

  


  
Cost

  
O(n log n) typically, but O(n²) in the worst case — and this implementation walks right into it. Because the pivot is always the first element, an already-sorted input partitions into “nothing” and “everything” every time, making the recursion n levels deep instead of log n. Production implementations pick a random pivot, or the median of three, to avoid exactly this.

The signature differences are the borrow-versus-consume distinction showing up in real code: bubble and quick take &mut [T] and edit what you already own; merge takes Vec<T> by value and produces something new.
